# 28 — the reference's own strategy in TinyGrad, and where the abstraction ends

## 0. The answer, first

1. **Reproducing the reference's *strategy* — its maintained flat address, its
   unsigned bounds fold, its one-load-per-step gather, its LUT epilogue, its
   split camera-basis kernel — changes the generated code by very little.** All
   five are in TinyGrad's normal vocabulary. The faithful port and the naive
   port agree bit-for-bit on the observation and on every per-pixel DDA step
   count, and in the fused regime they are within 2.4% of each other on static
   instructions (55 112 against 54 896 for the DDA-shaped programs alone is a
   0.4% difference; the whole-graph totals differ by the two extra layout
   kernels, §5.2). `MEASURED`, §4, §5.1, §5.2.

2. **The techniques that are not in TinyGrad's vocabulary are all in the same
   place: the register-file budget for the DDA body.** The reference keeps a
   **16-copy static window** of the step body live (`kernels.cu:681-687`), and
   ptxas allocates 16 *byte-identical* copies one at a time — 28 registers,
   zero spill, 776 instructions for the whole render. TinyGrad has no way to
   ask for a window: `OptOps.UNROLL` resolves to *reduce* axes only, the DDA
   has none, and no `ContextVar` sets the inline factor of an elementwise
   chain. `MEASURED`, §5.1 and §5.2.

3. **So this workload is an expressiveness problem, not a scheduling problem.**
   The gap the study has been measuring is not "a cost model could have found
   a better fusion"; it is that the *shape of the computation* — a
   register-budgeted partial unroll with a per-ray exit attached to each copy
   — is not a thing the framework's IR can be asked for, at any tensor size,
   through any of its documented knobs. The clean-vs-faithful delta is
   **1.467× in static instructions and 9.47× in spill at batch 1024** (§6.1),
   and on the card itself it is **1.067× in regime A and 3.47× / 3.67× in the
   fused regime** (§8.0) — a wall-clock cost 2.4× larger than the instruction
   count suggests, because the fused kernel is memory-bound on the very spill
   `addr_bits=64` creates. The delta is *entirely* the price of asking for the
   reference's 64-bit index inside TinyGrad: the fidelity switch that exists
   costs a factor of ten in spill and, measured on the card, a factor of 3.5 in
   wall clock, while buying nothing, because TinyGrad's index lowering narrows
   the 64-bit address at the load anyway; the switch that would buy the
   reference's performance does not exist.

4. **The load-count gap is smaller than the study has been saying, and the
   correction is by measurement.** `notes/19` §4.2(d)'s 4× redundant voxel load
   is a **regime-A** phenomenon, and regime A's grid puts 64 threads on one
   pixel, so the per-pixel figure there is 256. In regime B — the fused kernel,
   the one that gets timed — each step issues **one** load at a **different**
   address: **1.000 loads per pixel per step against the reference's
   1.000**. The 4× does not survive fusion. `MEASURED`, §5.1a and §9.3.

5. **Two of the three most-cited "optimisations" in the reference are not
   optimisations at all, and this arm can now say so from the SASS rather than
   by argument.** The shared-memory basis is 64 B and 1 barrier against a
   416-byte broadcast L1 read: measurable in the SASS, and the boundary map
   closes the row rather than leaving it as a guess. The `__ldg` read-only
   path is *absent* from the generated code — `CUDARenderer` never emits
   `restrict`, and 0 of 1 187 generated byte loads at batch 8 and 0 of 1 027 at
   batch 1024 are on it, against 16 of 16 in the reference. `MEASURED`, §5.2
   and §6 rows 9 and 10.

## 1. What this arm is, and the mistake it exists to avoid

`minecu_tg/` wrote the DDA the way a person writes a vectorised raymarcher.
`minecu_tg_opt/` fixed the algorithmic half of that. Both are legitimate, and
between them they answer a question neither can answer alone: *how much of the
gap to the hand-written kernel is the compiler's?* The answer is "not
directly measurable from a comparison, because the comparison is measuring the
ports too" — which is what `notes/06-corrections-ledger.md` §5b is four weeks
of evidence for, in two unrelated subjects.

This arm is the third question. A hand-written kernel beats a DSL for one of
two reasons: the compiler failed to find something a cost model could find, or
the optimisation lives *below* the abstraction the DSL offers. Those have
opposite consequences, and comparing a hand-written kernel against a
well-written DSL program cannot separate them — the DSL program was free to
use the DSL's own ideas and only those.

**So: reproduce the reference's actual strategy, including the parts below the
abstraction, and record every place the abstraction has to be crossed.** Where
the boundary turns out to be *is* the result. A finding of "this kernel's
advantage needs manual register-budget control that TinyGrad has no way to
express" is worth more than any speed ratio, because it is a statement about
the abstraction rather than about one version of one compiler.

`Tensor.custom_kernel` is not used anywhere in `minecu_tg_faithful/`, no `.cu`
is written or compiled alongside it, and no PTX is inlined. Everything that
runs is CUDA that TinyGrad's own `CUDARenderer` emitted from a UOp graph
`render.py` built. `Tensor.custom_kernel` takes CUDA source and emits it
(`tinygrad/tensor.py:390`, `uop/ops.py:1191`), so using it would have
substituted the reference's own kernel for the experiment and measured
nothing.

## 2. The reference, re-derived

`MEASURED`, `minecu_tg_faithful/sass.py`, from
`minecu_ref/build/kernels_sm_75.sass` and `resources_sm_75.txt`. Nothing here
is transcribed from a note; `notes/19` §4 quotes the same figures and the
re-derivation is what makes them usable as a baseline.

```
kernel            _Z28render_kernel_uint8_prebasisPKaPKfPhiiiiiii   (kernels.cu:568)
instructions      776
registers         28      spill stores 0   spill loads 0   stack frame 0
barriers          1       smem 64 B       cmem 404 + 8
BSSY / BSYNC      33      BRA 82           BREAK 16
byte loads        16 LDG.E.S8.CONSTANT.SYS  (+1 LDG.E.CONSTANT.SYS in the prologue)
DDA step body     load-to-load spacing exactly 38, fifteen times
                  38 instructions per step = 6 IMAD + 3 SHF + 3 IADD3 + 2 IADD3.X
                  (address) + 2 ISETP.GE.U32.OR (bounds) + 3 FSETP (t_max)
                  + 1 LDG + 1 ISETP.GT + BREAK + 3 BRA + 2 BSSY + 2 BSYNC
                  + IADD3 (step) + IMAD.MOV (sentinel) + ISETP.GE (cap) + 2 BRA
longest exact-match run  594 instructions at period 38
```

Two things in that stream are the mechanism for everything that follows, and
both are measurements rather than readings of the source:

- **14 of the 38 instructions per step are address arithmetic** — 6 `IMAD`,
  3 `SHF.R.S32.HI` sign-extensions, 3 `IADD3` and 2 `IADD3.X` carries. That is
  the `long long` promotion of `b*wxyz + voxel_y*wxz + voxel_x*wz + voxel_z`
  (`kernels.cu:669`) recomputed per step. ptxas did **not** strength-reduce it
  to a pointer increment. The reference is doing 37% of its DDA step in address
  arithmetic, and a port that gets that down to one add is not copying the
  reference, it is beating it. This is the opposite of the reading in the
  corrections ledger's C-14 row, and it is the one place where this arm's
  numbers and that ledger's disagree in sign.
- **The 16 unrolled steps are byte-identical repeats** (exact-match run 594 at
  period 38). That is not a stylistic observation; it is what makes 28
  registers sufficient. ptxas allocates a body it can prove is a repeat one
  copy at a time. The generated side's 32 steps are not a repeat — longest
  exact-match run 629 at period 40, with 512 byte loads at a spacing that is
  *not* uniform — and the allocator treats the whole 27 448-instruction region
  as one problem.

## 3. The port

`minecu_tg_faithful/render.py`, read its module docstring first. The shape:

- **one `for k in range(max_steps)` loop with one body, in both modes.** The
  reference's `#pragma unroll 4` is a source directive nvcc implements;
  TinyGrad's equivalent of "unroll the loop" is "the graph contains the body
  `max_steps` times", which a Python `for` gives. What TinyGrad then does with
  that straight line — fuse it, split it, spill it — is the measurement, and
  neither mode decides it.
- **the cursor is an integer, cast once** (`kernels.cu:638`), and the flat
  address is maintained by a precomputed signed stride rather than rebuilt from
  three coordinates. This is T4, the technique `notes/06` §5b's C-14 row
  correctly identifies as *the reference's* shape, and
  `minecu_tg_opt/render.py`'s rewrite 3.
- **the bounds test is a countdown of steps-to-exit**, three integer compares
  on values that are already live. The reference re-derives it from the cursor
  every step and folds six signed compares into three `ISETP.GE.U32.OR` by
  casting to unsigned.
- **one gather, one address**, `flat[inside.where(addr, 0)]`. A tensor gather
  has no bounds check while the reference's test gates a pointer it never
  forms, so the index is `inside.where(addr, 0)`: memory safety, and `inside`
  — computed anyway — decides whether the byte counts.
- **`live = hit < 0` is the per-ray early exit, as a mask**, where the
  reference has a `goto`. This is the crossing with no clean form, and it is
  in both modes because there is nothing else to write.
- **the colour store is a 14-row gather** (AIR → row 13), where the reference
  branches. Disclosed, not hidden: a tensor program has no `if` on a tensor.

### 3.2 The gaps, which are the result

Five techniques in the reference have **no TinyGrad lever at all**. They are
recorded as gaps with the reason, not worked around, and the port keeps the
nearest expressible approximation, labelled.

| gap | the reference | why there is no lever |
|---|---|---|
| `EARLY_EXIT` | `kernels.cu:670` `goto done_pre`, 33 `BSSY`/`BSYNC` | a per-ray trip count is not a fixed-size tensor, and `Ops` has no `WHILE`/`LOOP`/`REPEAT`; the only control flow is `IF`/`ENDIF`, synthesised for gated stores at `codegen/__init__.py:402-407` and unreachable from the Tensor API |
| `UNROLL_FACTOR` | `kernels.cu:681-687`, 16 copies, spacing 38 | `OptOps.UNROLL` (`codegen/opt/__init__.py:7`) resolves through `unrollable_dims` (`postrange.py:113`) and is gated on `if k.unrollable_dims` (`heuristic.py:139-152`); the DDA has no reduce axis. `BEAM` searches the same `Opt` space and would not find it either, because the action does not exist in the space |
| `FUSE_AT_REGISTER_BUDGET` | 28 registers, 0 spill, `resources_sm_75.txt` | `__launch_bounds__` is derived from the product of the local dims (`renderer/cstyle.py:156-157, 411`); there is no `maxrregcount` path. `MAX_KERNEL_BUFFERS` counts *arguments* (`rangeify.py:371`), not registers. `NOLOCALS` changes launch geometry, not the allocator |
| `RESTRICT_ON` | `__restrict__` on 81 parameters, 16 of 17 loads on the read-only path | `CUDARenderer` leaves `buffer_suffix` empty (`renderer/cstyle.py:121`); only `ClangRenderer` sets `" restrict"` (`cstyle.py:269`). No `ContextVar`, no dtype, no shape |
| `USE_FAST_MATH` | `nvcc -O3 --use_fast_math` (`minecu_ref/build.sh`) | `NVRTCCompiler.compile_options` is `[--gpu-architecture, -I…, --minimal]` and nothing else (`runtime/support/compiler_cuda.py:46-56`). No `ContextVar` and no renderer knob |

`USE_FAST_MATH` deserves a note about *why* it is a gap and not a flag.
Matching it would change the numerics of the computation being compared: the
generated source is `1/sqrt(...)` where the reference is `rsqrtf`, and under
`--use_fast_math` those differ. Turning the port's numerics to match is not a
crossing, it is a different experiment with a different observation, and the
honest form of the finding is "TinyGrad does not expose this and here is what
it costs" rather than a switch that quietly changes the answer.

Two more techniques are recorded as *present but inert*, so their rows exist
and are closed rather than inherited: the env-per-block 2-D grid (`GRID_AS_ENV`,
which exists only to enable the shared basis) and the AABB early-out
(`ray_intersects_aabb`, `kernels.cu:51-79`, which is in `render_kernel` and
**not** in `render_kernel_uint8_prebasis` — the kernel this arm ports;
`notes/18` entry 18 prices the other one).

`minecu_tg_faithful/verify.py`, this host, CPU, no GPU. `run.sh` refuses to
push if it fails. Exit code 0, **114 checks, 0 failures**.

```
  clean/addr64/uint1  [PASS] x4
  clean/addr64/uint0  [PASS] x4
  faithful/addr32/uint1 [PASS] x4   faithful/addr32/uint0 [PASS] x4
  faithful/addr64/uint1 [PASS] x4   faithful/addr64/uint0 [PASS] x4
  [PASS] count_steps=True does not change the observation
  [PASS] a shared camera gives identical envs at batch [1, 5]
  [PASS] per-env cameras: 5 distinct images at a +-1.2 rad yaw spread,
         each matching its own float64 reference
```

The eight configurations are both modes × both address widths × both bounds
folds, and the full sweep runs on the first shape only — the other four shapes
check both modes and both folds, because spending 20 minutes re-confirming that
a signed and an unsigned compare agree on a different world size is not a better
gate. 114 = 3 world checks + 5 shapes × (4 checks per variant + 3 shape-level
checks), the first shape carrying 8 variants and the other four carrying 4 each.

**This count was 82 and is now 114**, because the four non-first shapes were
drawing their variants from the same mode-major slice described in §8.5(a) and
so were rendering `clean` twice where they should have rendered both modes.
Nothing about the port's verification rests on the fix — the first shape always
ran the full eight — but the number this note quotes did, so it is corrected
here rather than left.

**The load-bearing check is the fourth of each group: the per-pixel DDA step
count is *identical* to the naive port's, in all eight configurations.** The
image is a lossy readout — many step counts collapse to the same block colour
— so the step count is the sharper instrument, and it is tie-sensitive in
exactly the way that would expose a changed traversal. A crossing that moved a
traversal decision would show here even if the image were unchanged. It does
not.

The 2/1024 disagreements against float64 at world 32 and 48 (99.80%, mean
16.029 vs 16.027) are float32-vs-float64 rounding of `t_max` at exact ties and
are shared with the naive port, which has the same mean to three decimals. The
gate is `--min-step-agree 0.99`.

**A passing correctness gate is not performance evidence, and not evidence the
baseline was the right one** (`notes/06` §5b: two large comparisons in this
project were invalidated by porting the wrong thing, and both ports were
*correct*, which is why nothing caught them). So this is stated as what it is:
the two modes compute the same thing, and the difference between them is
therefore attributable to the crossings. The reason the baseline is the right
one is §2: the port now *is* the reference's strategy, address arithmetic
included, and its per-step shape is on the record in `sass.py` rather than in a
prose description of what the reference does.

### 5.1a Loads per pixel per step, and why "4×" and "256×" are the same number

| | grid × block | threads per pixel | loads per **thread**-step | loads per **pixel**-step | int8 state stored per pixel-step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| reference | 128 threads | 1 | 1 | **1.000** | **0** |
| regime A, naive | (8,32,16) × (8,16) | **64** | 4 (same address) | **256.0** | **1 024 B** |
| regime A, faithful clean | (8,16,32) × (8,16) | **64** | 4 (same address) | **256.0** | **1 024 B** |
| regime A, faithful faithful | (8,16,32) × (8,16) | **64** | 4 (same address) | **256.0** | **1 024 B** |
| regime B, naive | (512) × (2,8,8) | 1/16 | 1 (distinct address) | **1.000** | 0 |
| regime B, faithful clean | (512) × (2,8,8) | 1/16 | 1 (distinct address) | **1.000** | 0 |
| regime B, faithful faithful | (512) × (2,8,8) | 1/16 | 1 (distinct address) | **1.000** | 0 |

**This corrects the scope of `notes/19` gap 7.** The "4 predicated loads of
one address per step" is real and it is a **regime-A** phenomenon, and regime
A's grid puts **64 threads on one pixel**, so 4 × 64 = **256 loads per pixel
per step**. In regime B — the fused kernel, the one that gets timed — the grid
is 16 pixels per thread and each step issues **one** load at a **different**
address: **1.000 loads per pixel per step, the same as the reference.** The
4× redundancy does not survive fusion.

So the load-count gap does not exist in the regime where the ratio is taken.
The gaps that do survive there are the register budget (§5.2) and the missing
window (§7). This is a scope correction to a published row, made by
measurement, and it moves the weight of the argument off the load count and
onto the register file — which is where §7 puts it anyway.

**Three things to read off, and one of them contradicts a number in the study.**

- **`BSSY` 33 → 0 in regime B, 33 → 21/31 in regime A.** The reference's
  per-ray early exit costs 33 divergence-sync pairs and stops a ray that has
  hit. The generated side has none and does *more total work*: every ray runs
  all 32 steps against a measured mean of 26.4 (`notes/19` §1.3), a 1.21×
  work term in the reference's favour. This is a number that looks like a
  regression and is not — the generated code is cheaper per step precisely
  because it has no per-ray exit to synchronise on, which is T6.
- **The read-only load path is gone, entirely.** 16 of the reference's 16 byte
  loads are `LDG.E.S8.CONSTANT.SYS`; the generated side's 1 187 are
  `LDG.E.SYS` or `LDG.E.U8.SYS`, none on the read-only path. That is
  `CUDARenderer.buffer_suffix` being empty (`renderer/cstyle.py:121`) against
  `ClangRenderer`'s `" restrict"` (`cstyle.py:269`), and there is no knob.
  Whether it costs *time* on sm_75 is not measured here; what is measured is
  that the SASS difference exists and that no lever reaches it.
- **The load *count* is fine in regime B, and the spacing is not.** One load
  per pixel per step, same as the reference (§5.1a). But the reference's 16
  loads sit at a spacing of exactly 38, fifteen times, while the fused kernel's
  512 are at a spacing of 12–20 that is **not uniform** and its longest
  exact-match run is 629 instructions at period 40 — a *fragment*, not a
  window. **There is no 16-wide static window anywhere in the generated code,
  in either mode, at either batch.** That is the finding, stated as a
  measurement of an absence.

**One thing this arm got wrong first, recorded because it would have changed
the verdict.** The first reading of the regime-A census was "256 loads per
pixel per step against the reference's 1", and the obvious conclusion was that
the load path was a 256× problem. It is not: 256 = 4 predicated loads × 64
threads-per-pixel, and the 64 comes from regime A's grid, not from the DDA. In
regime B the grid is 16 pixels per thread and the figure is **1.000** (§5.1a).
The load count was never the problem; the load *schedule* and the register
budget are. A per-thread number and a per-pixel number are the same measurement
at different denominators, and `notes/06`'s rule about not dividing by the
wrong thing applies to instruction counts as well as to throughput.

`NOLOCALS` is the closest thing to a lever for the shared-memory basis (T2),
and the measurement says it buys nothing: the DDA has no reduce axis for the
shared buffer it would otherwise create. It is therefore **off in both modes**,
and the reason is written down in `crossings.py`. Turning it on to *look*
faithful would be a costume.

`MAX_KERNEL_BUFFERS` is the sharpest negative result in the table. It is the
only knob in TinyGrad that splits a fusion, and it splits on *buffer count* —
`if len(bufs) > MAX_BUFS - 1` (`rangeify.py:371`) — not on registers, not on
instruction count, not on a live-set estimate. A kernel that wants a register
budget and a kernel that wants few arguments are different problems, and the
framework has the second one covered.

The two right-hand columns are the point. In regime A the clock lands within
2.3% of the instruction ratio — the crossings really are nearly free when
nothing is spilled. In regime B the clock sees **2.4× more than the instruction
stream does**, and the difference is the 9.47× spill: the fused kernel is
memory-bound on its own local traffic, which no instruction count can see. So
**1.467× is the honest lower bound on this crossing's cost, and it is 2.4× too
small.**

**Both deltas are one crossing.** The only difference between the modes is
`addr_bits`: 64 in `faithful`, 32 in `clean`, because that is the one fidelity
switch TinyGrad's vocabulary can reach, and `unsigned_bounds` is on in both
(it is the reference's form and it is free). So the delta is *the cost of
asking for the reference's 64-bit index inside TinyGrad* — and it is not small:
**1.467× the instructions, 9.47× the spill, 3.47× the wall clock**, because
TinyGrad's index lowering (`uop/weak.py:74-81`) narrows the long index at the
load anyway, so the 64-bit arithmetic is pure added cost with no added reach at
world 16.

That is the cleanest statement of the boundary available: **the one crossing
that exists costs 9.5× the spill and 3.5× the clock, and the crossings that
would buy the reference's performance do not exist.**

## 7. The single technique that most resists the abstraction

**The register-budgeted partial unroll: a fixed-width window of the DDA body
kept live in registers (T7), with a per-ray early exit attached to each copy
(T6).** Not the shared memory, not the `__ldg`, not the 64-bit index, not the
trig hoist — all of those are either free, absent, or expressible. This one is
structural, and the two halves are structural for the same reason: both are
statements about a *fixed-size* program with a *data-dependent* trip count,
and a tensor IR whose axes are shapes cannot say either.

The evidence that it is expressiveness and not search is four independent
measurements pointing the same way:

1. **`UNROLL_FACTOR` is a gap, not a knob.** `OptOps.UNROLL` resolves through
   `unrollable_dims` and the DDA has no reduce axis, so the action does not
   exist in the space `BEAM` searches. A cost model cannot find a move that is
   not in its move set. That is the definition of class (i), and it is a
   stronger statement than "the cost model did not find it".
2. **`MAX_KERNEL_BUFFERS` is the only fusion-splitting lever, and it splits on
   the wrong axis.** `if len(bufs) > MAX_BUFS - 1` (`rangeify.py:371`) is a
   count of *arguments*. Measured SAME at 2, 4, 8 and 64. A kernel that wants
   a register budget and a kernel that wants few arguments are different
   problems, and the framework has the second covered.
3. **The load spacing, measured.** The reference: 16 byte loads at a spacing of
   exactly 38, fifteen times, exact-match run 594 at period 38. The fused
   kernel at batch 1024: 512 byte loads at a **non-uniform** spacing of 12–20,
   exact-match run 629 at period 40 — a fragment, not a window. **There is no
   16-wide static window in the generated code at either batch, in either
   mode.** The absence is a measurement, not an inference from the source. Note
   that the *count* is not the problem: 1.000 loads per pixel per step, the
   same as the reference (§5.1a). The load count is the same; the load
   *schedule* is not.
4. **The consequence is the register file.** 28 registers and zero spill
   against 255 registers (the ceiling) and 3 572 B of spill in `clean`, 168
   and 33 824 B in `faithful`. `ptxas` allocated the reference's 16 identical
   copies one at a time *because they were identical*; it allocates the fused
   region as one problem because it is one straight-line region of 27 448
   instructions. Same allocator, same `nvcc 12.9`, same `-arch=sm_75`.

So: **expressiveness, class (i).** And the practical consequence is sharper
than a speed ratio. A scheduling problem has a knob; this one does not, and
the nearest available schedule knobs — `NOOPT`, `NOLOCALS`,
`MAX_KERNEL_BUFFERS` — move the register count and the launch geometry but
cannot produce a window, because a window is not a shape TinyGrad's IR has a
notion of. The one thing that would fix it is a new IR node: "unroll this
elementwise chain to width N and keep the N bodies in one kernel", which is a
*language* change rather than a pass change. `notes/19` §7's three
experiments — raise the fusion threshold, add a register-pressure check, add a
load-once gather — are all pass changes, and this arm's evidence is that all
three would land on the wrong side of the boundary.

## 8. Timings on the T4

`minecu_tg_faithful/battery.py` via `minecu_tg_faithful/kaggle/run.sh`: one
push, one T4, the hand-written baseline and both modes in the same process
tree, render-against-render at equal batch, world 16³, res 32×32, ground
`world/4`, `max_steps=32`, batches 512/1024/2048.

**The category rules, enforced in the code rather than the prose.** The
reference's *whole-step* figure is printed and is never a denominator: a whole
step is 7 launches, five of them state updates, and `4.55 M env-steps/s` is
context. Both sides' *renders* are one launch's worth of the same work, same

**The crossing cost is regime-specific, and the regimes are the story.**

* **Regime A (batch 512, 42 kernels): 1.067×.** The offline static instruction
  ratio at that shape is 41 080 / 39 352 = **1.044×** (§5.1). The wall clock
  lands within 2.3% of the static ratio. Here the crossings are close to free,
  and the reason is visible in the kernel count: 42 launches, none of them a
  27 000-instruction fused body, so there is no register pressure to spill.
* **Regime B (batch 1024 and 2048, 10 kernels, the fused one): 3.47× and

  kernel is memory-bound on its own spill, and `addr_bits=64` is what makes it
  spill.

That is the sharpest form of the §6.1 result: the 64-bit index costs 1.467× in
instructions and 3.47× in wall clock, and **the difference between those two
numbers is the spill**, which no static instruction count can see. The
instruction ratio is the honest lower bound on the crossing's cost and it is
2.4× too small.

**The observation is byte-identical across the modes at every batch**
(`same obs yes`), and the DDA workload is identical too — 16.608 mean
steps/pixel in all six measurements. So the crossings did not change the
computation, and the 1.067× / 3.47× are the cost of expressing the same
computation in the reference's terms.

**Two things this table does not say, stated because they are visible in the
raw record.** The host-side `build + realize + synchronize` is 416–581 ms
against 3–21 ms of device time — the framework's graph tracing dominates
end-to-end by 25–140×, and it is printed on its own line and marked not
comparable, because the reference pays no such cost. And `clean/ref` improves
from 110× at batch 512 to 10.0× at batch 2048: the naive-vs-faithful porting
style is not uniformly bad, and quoting the worst batch would misrepresent it in
the *port's* disfavour just as quoting the best would in the *framework's*.

### 8.1 Failures, and what each one was actually about

Nine pushes have been made and seven failed. Every one of them is about the
harness rather than the port, and they are the kind of failure a summary hides:

1. **The first push failed on the card's missing `clang`.** The correctness gate
   ran with `DEV=CPU`, and tinygrad's CPU backend shells out to `clang`
   (`ClangRenderer`, `renderer/cstyle.py:255`), which the T4 image does not
   have. The same failure `notes/19` §4.0 recorded. The fix — verify on
   `DEV=CUDA` — is not a workaround: it checks the render on the device every
   timing is taken on, against the same float64 reference.
2. **The second push printed `DEV=CUDA` in its banner and still ran on the
   CPU.** `tinygrad.helpers` reads `DEV` into a `ContextVar` **at import time**
   (`ContextVar.__init__`, `helpers.py:182`), so a value set after the first
   `import tinygrad` changes nothing — and the banner, which printed
   `os.environ`, was reporting the intent rather than the fact. The gate now
   pre-scans `--dev` above the tinygrad import and prints
   `Device.DEFAULT` from the framework itself, because a banner that can
   disagree with reality is worse than no banner: it says `DEV=CUDA` over a
   CPU run and the traceback that follows is about `clang`.

Both are recorded here rather than quietly fixed because the second one is a
general trap for this repo's harnesses: any script that sets `DEV` after
importing tinygrad prints a device it did not use.

3. **A third push was refused outright: `Maximum batch GPU session count of 2
   reached`.** The account allows two concurrent GPU sessions and the other
   lanes in this repo are pushing at the same time. Nothing was uploaded and no
   accelerator time was spent. `run.sh` now recognises that message and exits
   75 with the quota as its stated cause, rather than proceeding to a poll
   loop that would have spun for 90 minutes against a run that was never
   queued.
4. **The fourth and fifth pushes went `RUNNING`, then `CANCEL`, and the status
   said quota again. It was not the quota: this arm was killing its own runs.**
   `kaggle kernels push -t` is documented in the CLI's own help as *"Limit the
   run time of a kernel to the given number of seconds"*, and this arm passed
   `SESSION_MIN=50` there unscaled — **a fifty-second session**. The card's own
   log says so in one line:

   ```
   nbclient.exceptions.CellTimeoutError: A cell timed out while it was
   executed, after 50 seconds.
   ```

   at t = 59.6 s, immediately after the payload hash-check (t = 9.3 s) and
   `pip install tinygrad` (t = 17.1 s) had both finished and while the on-card
   correctness gate was still running. The two other lanes that have landed a
   run on this account got the units right — `minecu_tg/kaggle/run.sh:318`
   passes `-t $((SESSION_MIN * 60))` — and their fetched notebook
   (`out/minecu_tg/20260928-221859/`) ran **2 532 s inside a single code cell**,
   which is the positive control: there is no per-cell timeout on this platform,
   so a cell that outlives the run cap is the only explanation that fits. The
   fix is `SESSION_MIN=75` converted to seconds at the call site, and a
   floor check that refuses to push a cap under an hour.

   Two things are worth separating here. The **status vocabulary** is lossy:
   `CANCEL` is what a self-imposed 50-second cap looks like from outside, and it
   is indistinguishable at the API from a quota kill or a supersede. The
   **diagnosis** was available the whole time — in the card's log, one
   `kaggle kernels logs` call away — and `run.sh` was polling instead of
   fetching. It now fetches the card's log on `CANCEL*`, greps it for
   `CellTimeoutError`, and reports which of the two causes it is rather than
   guessing from the status. Note also what this failure is *not*: it is not a
   correctness failure. The gate had not returned, so nothing about the port was
   tested either way.

5. **The gate was checking half of what it claimed, and the battery would not
   have run at all.** Both found while budgeting the on-card run, neither by
   any measurement in this note.

   **(a) `--quick` never rendered in `faithful` mode.** It selected its
   variants with `variants()[:2]` on a mode-major list of eight, which is
   `clean/addr32/uint1` and `clean/addr32/uint0` — the same mode twice. And
   `--quick` is the gate `run.sh` runs before it will push anything *and* the
   gate the card runs. The full gate did cover both modes on its first shape, so
   the port was verified; but the pre-push gate was not testing the thing it is
   named for. A subset of a sweep has to be keyed on an axis, not sliced by
   position: `quick_variants()` now selects by `addr_bits`, which keeps both
   bounds folds (covered before) and adds both modes (covered before neither),
   so it is a strict superset at the same render count. Two asserts now hold the
   ordering and the coverage, so a future edit cannot silently drop a mode
   again. Quick is now 41 checks over 2 configurations; the full gate is 114
   over 8, up from 82 — §4 says why.

   **(b) `battery.py` had a `NameError` on its first batch row.** An f-string
   read a bare `max_steps` where the local is `a.max_steps`. It passed the host
   gate because the host gate never ran `battery.py` — the gate verified the
   *port* and the card runs the *battery*, and nothing between the two was ever
   executed on this host. On the card it would have died after the hash-check,
   the install, the build and the on-card gate, with the timing stage begun and
   no timing produced.

   The fix is not the typo. A gate that does not run the code the push will run
   is not a gate, so `run.sh` now runs `battery.py` before it pushes — batch 1
   and 2, 8×8, one iteration, no warmup, five seconds, both modes, every branch
   that matters. And `make_notebook.py` refuses to write a notebook whose cell
   reads a name it does not already have bound (`undefined_names`): the cell is
   assembled by string substitution, so a name can fall out of sync with the
   module that fills it in and be syntactically perfect while raising
   `NameError` on the card, which `compile()` cannot see.

   **(c) And the check itself was wrong the first time, which is worth saying.**
   `undefined_names` originally collected bindings from the whole file
   regardless of order. It passed a cell whose first statement was a `say(…)`
   call placed three lines *above* `def say` — a `NameError` on the card's very
   first output line, thirteen seconds into a paid-for session, found only
   because that push's log was read rather than summarised. A name being bound
   somewhere in a file is not the same as it being bound before it is used. The
   check now makes two passes: "never bound anywhere", and "bound after its
   use at module level", with `except … as e` modelled as binding-then-deleting,
   comprehension targets bound before the element that reads them, `global`
   seeded from the top, and a star import switching the check off rather than
   producing noise. Seventeen cases are checked by hand against it, including
   the two real bugs. A checker that cries wolf gets ignored, and one that
   waves through a real defect is worse than not having written it, so both
   directions have to be right.

   All three defects were in the harness. None had produced a wrong number:
   they would have produced *no* number, at full price, three times.

6. **Push 7 got all the way through the on-card gate and died building the
   hand-written side.** `refdrv.cu` does `#include MINECU_KERNELS`, which
   defaults to `"kernels.cu"`; `#include` resolves relative to the *including*
   file, which is `minecu_tg/kaggle/`, while the notebook writes `kernels.cu` to
   `minecu_ref/src/`. So the compile failed with

   ```
   refdrv.cu:36:24: fatal error: kernels.cu: No such file or directory
   ```

   on a file that is correct, over a path this stage owns. `refdrv.cu:33-38`
   documents the override for exactly this (`-DMINECU_KERNELS=`), and the two
   lanes that have landed a run both pass it — `minecu_tg/kaggle/make_notebook.py:210`
   — which is what a working neighbour is for.

   The interesting part is what this says about the gate. `run.sh` had a
   payload-layout pre-flight and a correctness gate, and **neither one compiled
   anything**, because there is no nvcc on this host. So the one stage most
   likely to be wrong in a way only the card can show — a path that resolves
   differently under `/kaggle/working` — was the one stage with no local
   witness at all. `run.sh` now compiles the notebook's exact nvcc command in
   the `archlab/nvcc:12.9` container before it pushes, and checks the result
   carries no sm_80+ opcodes; that check is the same one `minecu_tg/kaggle/run.sh:237`
   arrived at from the opposite direction. It cost one container start and
   found the next two defects in the wrapper itself, both recorded here
   because both are ways a pre-flight silently stops being one: a pipeline
   whose exit status came from `sed` rather than from `docker`, and a
   `VAR="$(cmd)"` under `set -e` that aborted the push on a pre-flight which had
   just *passed*, because `grep -c` exits 1 on the zero matches that constitute
   success.

7. **Push 8 ran to completion and reported that the port is thirteen times
   faster than the hand-written CUDA.** It is the most valuable thing this arm
   produced on the card, and it is worth writing out in full because the number
   was *false*, and the way it was false is a property of the framework rather
   than of this port.

   | batch | reference (cudaEvent, µs) | port as reported (ms) | ratio |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 512 | 152.1 | 0.012 | **0.08×** |
   | 1024 | 262.1 | 0.011 | **0.04×** |
   | 2048 | 507.3 | 0.011 | **0.02×** |

   A ratio that improves as the batch grows, on a port that offline SASS puts at
   39 352 static instructions against the reference's 776 — §5.1 — is not a
   result, it is a shape that says the clock is not attached to the work. The
   cause is one line: `time_graph` called `obs.realize()` in a loop, and
   **`realize()` on an already-realized tensor returns immediately without
   launching anything** (`minecu_tg/bench.py:247-249` names this and times the
   graph *before* realizing it, for exactly this reason). The first warm-up
   iteration consumed the graph; the ten timed iterations measured the cost of
   calling a Python method and synchronizing an already-idle device. The 11 µs
   is host overhead.

   Nothing about it looked wrong on the page: three batches, two modes, clean
   and faithful agreeing to within 10 %, `same obs True` at every batch, a
   DDA workload line, a plausible-looking kernel count. The observation digests
   really were equal, because those *were* computed by a fresh graph — the
   digest path was correct and the timing path was not, in the same function,
   six lines apart.

   The fix is to time the thing rather than its absence:
   `minecu_tg.bench.linear_device_time_ms` walks the compiled `LINEAR` and sums
   `time_call` over each `CALL`, on a graph that is verified unrealized at entry
   (`time_graph` now raises otherwise). `minecu_tg/bench.py` travels in the
   notebook unmodified and is sha256-checked like every other payload, so the
   two arms' device times come out of one code path rather than being asserted
   comparable. The host-side `build + realize + synchronize` figure is now
   reported on its own line and explicitly marked not comparable, because it
   contains graph tracing that the reference does not pay.

   Two lessons, both of which the study has form for in `notes/06`: a number
   that is *too good* deserves the same suspicion as one that is too bad, and a
   harness defect that produces a wrong number is worse than one that produces
   none, because a wrong number gets quoted. This one was caught only because
   the ratio moved the wrong way with batch size — a shape, not a threshold.
   The defensive change is structural rather than a plausibility bound: the
   timing function now refuses a realized tensor rather than trusting its
   caller.

### 9.2 A correction this arm makes to `notes/06` §5b

The C-14 row says the reference "advances a precomputed address — which is
what Amanatides–Woo *is*", and prices the port's per-step address arithmetic as
9.73× of the published 47.2×. **The SASS says the opposite.** The reference
rebuilds `b*wxyz + voxel_y*wxz + voxel_x*wz + voxel_z` in `long long` on every
step — 6 `IMAD`, 3 `SHF` sign-extensions, 3 `IADD3` and 2 `IADD3.X` carries,
**14 of the 38 instructions in its DDA step** — and ptxas did not strength-
reduce it. The port that maintains an address is not restoring fidelity to the
reference; it is beating it on an axis the reference is weak on.

This does not rescue the 47.2×. It changes what the number is *about*: the
remaining difference is not "the port recomputed the address" but the
register-budgeted window and the per-ray exit, which is a smaller and a
sharper claim than the one the ledger makes. The correct form of C-14 is that
the naive port is worse than the reference in a way the reference is also bad
at, and that the surviving gap is the abstraction boundary in §7.

### 9.3 A second correction, to `notes/19` gap 7: the 4× load is regime A only

`load_accounting.py`, §5.1a. The 4 predicated byte loads of one address per
step are real, and they are a **regime-A** phenomenon; regime A's grid is 64
threads per pixel, so the per-pixel figure is 256. In regime B — the fused
kernel, the one that gets timed — the grid is 16 pixels per thread and each
step issues exactly **one** load at a **different** address, giving **1.000
loads per pixel per step against the reference's 1.000**.

Two consequences. First, `notes/19` §4.2(d)'s "517 global loads in regime B,
against 16" is a whole-program count being compared against a per-program one;
the like-for-like per-pixel-per-step figures are 1.000 against 1.000. Second,
the load count drops out of the argument for the regime that is timed, which
leaves the register budget and the missing unroll window — which is where §7
puts the answer, and where it now has to be, because the load argument is gone.

### 9.4 What this arm did not establish

- **The reference's own device-side DDA step count**, and so the one-sidedness
  of the workload check in §8.0. `refdrv` links no `dda_probe_kernel`, so the
  16.608 mean steps/pixel in §8.0 is measured by this port's own graph and the
  reference's is absent rather than substituted. Linking
  `minecu_ref/bench/bench.cu`'s probe is the cheapest way to close it.
- **Whether the reference's dumped observation equals this port's, on the
  card.** §8.0's digest equality is between the two *modes* of this port.
  `notes/19` §1.2 makes the reference-side comparison on the reference's own
  settled state; this arm did not re-derive it and does not claim it.
- **Whether the reference's 64 threads/pixel grid in regime A is itself a
  TinyGrad bug or a consequence of the masked formulation's extra buffers.**
  The 4 predicated loads of one address are structural to the masked
  formulation — four `bool` consumers under four predicates the compiler
  cannot relate (`notes/19` §3.2) — but the 64-thread replication is a
  schedule decision, and `MAX_KERNEL_BUFFERS` is the only knob that influences
  it and is a no-op here. Splitting those two is the cheapest next experiment
  in this lane and is not done.
- **Whether a `while`-style IR node would fix it.** §7 argues the fix is a
  language change, not a pass change. That is an argument from the absence of
  a lever, and it is as strong as the argument can be without building the
  node. It is not a proof that the node would work.
